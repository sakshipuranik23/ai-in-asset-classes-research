Research Notes

⚠ Critical Risk — The Crowded Trade Feedback Loop
When Every AI Predicts Steepening, the Curve Flattens
The mechanism is precise: every major AI yield curve model predicts the same curve steepening. Every fund manager using these models buys the long end of the curve simultaneously, driving long-end yields down. The collective buying pressure flattens the curve — the opposite of what was predicted. The models then read the flattening as a new input, generate revised predictions, trigger new trades, and the feedback loop compounds into a Model Meltdown — cascading model errors driven by the models themselves. The mitigations are deliberately decentralising: model heterogeneity (different institutions should use different feature sets and training windows to avoid identical predictions), position-size-adjusted signal delivery (large holders act on earlier, less precise signals rather than the same precise signal simultaneously), and confidence threshold gates that prevent automatic position-taking unless the model's confidence exceeds a much higher bar than historical norms — forcing human review in ambiguous conditions where crowding risk is highest.
⚠ Model Risk — The Black Swan Inversion
A Model That Never Saw 2022 Cannot Predict 2026
The 2022–2024 yield curve inversion — where 2-year Treasury yields exceeded 10-year yields by over 100 basis points for more than two years — was the most severe and sustained inversion in four decades. Most ML models trained on 2010–2021 data predicted inversions would be brief and self-correcting because that is what all the training data showed. They persisted for over two years. The failure mode is directional bias: the model confidently generates the wrong prediction because it is operating outside its training distribution without knowing it. The fix is multi-layered: the Bayesian Uncertainty Calibration step (Stage 4) is the primary architectural response — it widens confidence intervals when the model detects current conditions are dissimilar to training data, signalling that the prediction should be treated as low-confidence. The Macro Sentry (bottom layer) continuously checks whether incoming macro data matches any historical regime the model was trained on — flagging structural breaks as a separate alert before the model produces a biased prediction.
The Multi-Curve Complexity Problem
Why multi-curve mattersPost-LIBOR, four major risk-free rates operate simultaneously: SOFR (US), €STR (Eurozone), SONIA (UK), MIBOR (India). Each reflects different central bank regimes, credit characteristics, and liquidity conditions — they do not move in lockstep.
Basis RiskA model predicting the SOFR curve and €STR curve independently will produce spread predictions that imply arbitrage opportunities that do not exist in practice. The multi-curve model must maintain spread consistency across all four curves simultaneously — a substantially harder problem than single-curve prediction.
PCA + Deep LearningPCA reduces the yield curve to three latent factors (Level, Slope, Curvature) that explain ~99% of historical variance. Deep Learning then detects when the current curve is being driven by a fourth factor — typically a structural break — that PCA cannot capture within its three-factor framework.
Gaussian ProcessesProvide calibrated uncertainty estimates at each point on the curve — not just a single predicted yield, but a probability distribution. The 95% confidence interval band on the Curve Cockpit SVG is produced by the GP layer, not the neural network.
